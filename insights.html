Insights

Perspectives on AI-enabled product transformation, customer understanding, and the decisions that connect faster delivery to business results.

Why isn’t faster AI development producing better product outcomes?

Each team can deliver successfully while the product still fails the customer. AI can accelerate that pattern: more implementation moves forward while decisions affecting the complete customer experience remain unresolved.

Consider a product review. Engineering reports faster delivery. Design has simplified key screens. Product has shipped its priority features.

Yet customers still struggle with the problem the initiative was supposed to solve.

Everyone can demonstrate progress. The customer cannot experience it.

Atlassian's 2026 developer-experience survey reflects the broader gap: developers report faster delivery while most still experience the same or more workflow friction. [1]

Faster delivery can carry an unresolved problem further

Organizations divide work so teams can act with focus and expertise. Customers experience how those contributions work together.

An improvement can meet its acceptance criteria while leaving the customer's task unfinished. The gap might involve a business rule, a dependency or competing priorities that no individual team can resolve.

With faster implementation, more work can proceed around an untested interpretation of that gap. Other features begin to depend on it. By the time the issue reaches a cross-functional review, resolving it may require revisiting work that several teams considered complete.

Miro's CPTO, Jeff Chow, describes a related challenge: translating individual AI velocity into company-level impact requires faster cross-functional alignment and decision-making. [2]

Increasing delivery capacity has limited value when the customer outcome depends on a constraint that capacity cannot resolve.

Three improvements. One unfinished customer task.

Suppose an enterprise wants customers to complete account setup without contacting support.

Product removes unnecessary steps. Design makes the remaining steps easier to understand. Engineering automates provisioning.

All three improvements work.

But customers with a common account type still encounter an eligibility exception requiring manual approval. The interface is clearer and provisioning is faster; the customer still has to call.

Resolving that exception requires reconciling customer needs, business policy and operational risk. Additional interface or automation work may leave the obstacle intact.

The initiative needs a shared definition of success—customers completing setup independently—and someone empowered to negotiate the tradeoffs required to achieve it.

DORA's research guidance reinforces the importance of that customer focus: accelerating output without it can amplify existing weaknesses rather than improve the value delivered. [3]

Review the complete outcome

When AI output rises without comparable product progress, examine one important initiative:

  • Can customers now complete the task the investment was intended to improve?
  • Where do completed team contributions still leave a gap?
  • Who can resolve the conflicting priorities, rules or dependencies behind it?

Naming an accountable owner helps only if that person has the authority, evidence and cross-functional participation needed to act.

That gives leadership a basis for directing increased capacity toward the obstacle that matters. It also helps teams recognize when their individual work is complete but the initiative still needs a decision.

At the next review, ask: “What still prevents the customer from succeeding—and are we empowered to change it?”
